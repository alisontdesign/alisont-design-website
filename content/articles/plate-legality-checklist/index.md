Most plate offences in Singapore are not committed by criminals — they are committed by owners who never knew the rule. A frame bought online, a bulb that died quietly, a plate clipped on slightly crooked at a workshop. Here are the seven checks that cover nearly everything, each one quoted from the actual rules.

## 1. The colours match your plate type

LTA's [standard registration page](https://onemotoring.lta.gov.sg/content/onemotoring/home/buying/vehicle-types-and-registrations/car/standard_registration.html) allows two schemes for ordinary vehicles. Default: "The vehicle registration number should be in white lettering on a black background." If your plate is reflective material, the scheme flips: "For the front, the licence plate should have black lettering on a white background" and "For the back, the licence plate should have black lettering on a yellow background". Grey, chrome, neon or 'matte carbon' plate backgrounds are not on the list — but matte *finish* in a legal colour is fine, which is what our [matte on matte build](/catalogue/3d-matte-on-matte/) demonstrates.

## 2. The characters are the right size

Every character: "Letters and digits are 70 mm high, 50 mm wide, and 10 mm broad", with "The space between adjoining letters or between adjoining digits is 10 mm". Squeezed, shrunken or skinny fonts — the classic 'styled' plate — fail here first. If someone trimmed or reprinted your characters to fit a smaller plate, see our dedicated article on [trimming plates](/articles/trimming-number-plate-legal/).

## 3. The plate is flat and mounted right

The registration must "be exhibited on a flat rectangular plate" and sit low and upright: the rules require it to be displayed "not more than one metre from the ground in a vertical position" so every character reads squarely from behind and in front. Angled plate brackets (the 'slanted plate' look) fail the vertical requirement by design.

## 4. Nothing crowds the plate

Rule 19 is blunt: "No other figures or letters and no design advertisement or ornamentation shall be placed near the identification mark" in a way that makes it harder to read. Busy frames with flags, slogans or grills, and sticker clutter around the recess, all live on the wrong side of this line. Full breakdown in [the covers and frames guide](/articles/plate-covers-frames-legal-singapore/).

## 5. Nothing has been done to the plate surface

The tampering rule bans altering the plate so as to "cause the characters of the identification mark to be less easily distinguishable", and separately bans treatments that make characters "become or act as retroreflective characters" or that "prevent or impair the making of a true and clear photographic or recorded image". In practice: no tinted plate films, no reflective sprays, no prismatic 'anti-camera' coatings. The [custom plate legality guide](/articles/3d-plates-legal-singapore/) explains where legitimate custom work ends and this rule begins.

## 6. The plate is firmly attached

A plate swinging on one screw or dangling after a failed adhesive is its own breach: the rules require that where the mark is on a plate, "the flat rectangular plate must at all times be securely fastened to the motor vehicle". Proper screws, evenly, square — which is exactly how our [fitting service](/catalogue/removable-frameless-mounting-kit/) does it.

## 7. The rear plate is lit after dark

"Whenever a motor vehicle is on a road between sunset and sunrise, the identification mark exhibited at the back of the motor vehicle" must be "adequately illuminated as to render easily distinguishable every letter and figure of the identification mark". A dead plate lamp turns a perfectly legal plate into a non-compliant one every night. Full explanation in [the plate light rules article](/articles/plate-light-rules/).

## The one-line summary

Legal plates are boring on purpose: prescribed colours, prescribed characters, flat, low, clean, lit and screwed tight. Everything else is decoration of the *car*, not the *plate* — and that boundary is where enforcement lives.
